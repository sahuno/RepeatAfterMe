One read, many places

Young copies of a repeat are nearly identical. A short read from one copy fits the others equally well, so the aligner cannot tell where it came from, and may throw it away.

What STAR does with each read

STAR is the default aligner in nf-core/rnaseq and in the ENCODE RNA-seq pipeline. With its default settings it sorts every read by how many places it fits equally well.

1One alignment255Yes
2Both alignments, one marked primary3No
3–4All alignments, one marked primary1No
5–10All alignments, one marked primary0No
More than 10Nothing. Counted as "mapped to too many loci" in Log.final.outnoneNo, and neither does a multi-mapper count
Every starting match in more than 50 placesNothing. STAR never tries to align it; Log.final.out counts it as "unmapped: other"noneNo, and neither does a multi-mapper count

STAR begins each alignment from short exact matches between the read and the genome, called anchors, and by default it ignores an anchor found in more than 50 places. That limit is the table's last row. In a young subfamily every anchor is found in hundreds or thousands of places, so the read is never aligned and looks like an ordinary failed read.

The model above shows only the 10-place limit. MAPQ is the aligner's confidence that a read is in the right place. "Fits equally well" means within 1 point of the best score; one different letter costs 2 points, so a copy that differs by one letter does not count as a place.

Which subfamilies lose their reads

These numbers come from the real mouse and human genomes. From every subfamily, 2,000 reads were cut from the genome at random spots and aligned back with STAR. Each dot is one subfamily: how different its copies are from their original sequence, against the share of its reads that land in one place, the only reads a unique-only count keeps.

Where one subfamily's reads go

Tap a dot above, or find a subfamily by name. For each read length, one set of 2,000 reads is aligned twice: with STAR's default settings, and with both limits raised to 100.

What to do

  1. Do not test young subfamilies on unique reads alone. Keeping only MAPQ 255 does this, and so does featureCounts with its default settings, which do not count multi-mapping reads at all.
  2. Let the aligner keep more places. The TEtranscripts authors report that keeping up to 100 alignments per read is a good compromise between file size and recovering multi-mappers; with STAR they set --outFilterMultimapNmax and --winAnchorMultimapNmax both to 100. In the runs above, raising both limits rescues the reads that fit too many places, but most never-aligned reads of the youngest subfamilies stay never aligned, because their anchors sit in more than 100 places.
  3. Read the "unmapped: other" line in Log.final.out. It counts reads STAR never tried because of the anchor limit, along with bad reads such as adapter dimers or contamination, so a large value can come from young repeats as well as from poor sequencing. To tell the two apart, align again with a higher --winAnchorMultimapNmax and see whether the line shrinks.
  4. Then count the multi-mappers on purpose. Either add them up per subfamily, where it does not matter which copy a read came from (TEtranscripts does this), or share each read out between its places (featureCounts -M --fraction gives each place 1/n of a read; other tools estimate the split from the unique reads, compared in Counting tools).
  5. Name a single copy only when its reads fit that copy alone. A read that fits 500 copies tells you the subfamily is expressed and nothing about which copy.

TEtranscripts: Jin Y, Tam OH, Paniagua E, Hammell M (2015) Bioinformatics 31:3593–9; settings from the TEtranscripts README. featureCounts multi-mapping options: Subread users guide.
Real data: for every subfamily covering at least 1,000 bases (Simple_repeat and Low_complexity left out), 2,000 perfect single-end reads were cut from the primary chromosomes, each centred on a random base of the subfamily's rows (so near the edge of a short row a read runs into neighbouring DNA), and aligned with STAR 2.7.11b to indexes built from the GENCODE primary assemblies (no alternate haplotypes; chromosome lengths identical to the UCSC assemblies). Totals agree with STAR's Log.final.out in every run, and reads placed in one place landed where they were cut (all but 1 of 2,066,035 in mouse at 50 bases). Anchor limit: winAnchorMultimapNmax 50, "max number of loci anchors are allowed to map to", in the same parametersDefault file.
