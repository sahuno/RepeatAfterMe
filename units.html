Rows or subfamilies

Why does one subfamily fill thousands of rows of the mm10 repeat table, and do you test rows or subfamilies? A row is a piece of one insertion, and one insertion can be several rows. Testing subfamilies gives about a thousand tests that lose where the RNA came from; testing rows keeps the place but gives millions of features, most with no reads and several per insertion.

What one row is

RepeatMasker, the program that made the table, writes one line for each stretch of DNA that matches one repeat's consensus (the reconstructed original sequence). Its help page shows why one element can take several lines: "This is a sequence in which a Tigger1 DNA transposon has integrated into a MER7 DNA transposon copy. Subsequently two Alus integrated in the Tigger1 sequence." The Tigger1 appears as three lines, cut apart by the two Alus, and the MER7A as two, on either side of the Tigger1.

RepeatMasker's output files carry an ID column for this: "Fragments of a single element, separated from each other by subsequent insertions of other elements, deletions or recombinations, carry the same number" (RepeatMasker help text). The UCSC table keeps the rows, but its id column does not hold these numbers (on chr7 every row has the same id).

  • On this page, a row is one line of the table, and an insertion is the set of rows RepeatMasker gives one ID: its judgement that they are one element. A subfamily is a repeat name, such as L1Md_T.
  • Which copies and Counting tools call a row a copy, as the copy-level tools do.

Which unit to test

  1. A subfamily test adds up every row of the subfamily, so you test about a thousand features. TEtranscripts works this way: "quantification by TEtranscripts is limited to the family level" (Lanciano and Cristofari, p. 730). The sum loses the place: subfamily studies "are unable to distinguish TE-intrinsic expression via TE-derived regulatory sequences from TE-extrinsic expression due to TE inclusion in a longer transcript" (Yang et al. 2019; see Passenger or driver), and it cannot say which copies changed (Which copies).
  2. A row test keeps the place. SQuIRE's locus is one row: its code builds one name per table line from chromosome, start, end and repeat name. Expect most rows to have no reads: Yang et al. found subfamily-level expression came "from fewer than 7% of each subfamily's loci", and Lanciano and Cristofari note that "each locus contributes only minimally to the whole transcriptome" (p. 726).
  3. The number of rows costs less than it looks, but not nothing. DESeq2 "omits all genes with mean normalized counts below a filtering threshold from multiple testing adjustment", so rows with almost no reads do not count against you; the rows that do have reads still number far more than subfamilies, and "multiple testing adjustment tends to be associated with a loss of power" (Love et al. 2014). For the youngest families the row counts themselves are uncertain: "identifying the exact expressed loci remains approximate, particularly for the youngest TE families" (p. 731).
  4. One insertion is several features. A split L1, or an IAP with two LTRs and a body, enters a row test as several rows under different names, and its reads are divided among them. Before you call one copy changed, join its rows: by RepeatMasker's ID, from the .out files (UCSC bigZips chromOut.tar.gz for mm10), or by distance, as Teissandier et al. (2019) did, merging pieces of one copy "if the distance between them is less than 1000 bp". The two rules do not give the same insertions, so say which you used.
  5. Name the unit and the table version in your methods: "rows of the UCSC mm10 RepeatMasker table (open-3-3-0, library 20110920)" is a different feature set from insertions, and from another RepeatMasker run.
