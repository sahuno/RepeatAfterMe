Passenger or driver

An ERV copy inside a gene gets reads whenever the gene is transcribed, because the gene's unspliced RNA carries the copy along. A count over the copy cannot tell those passenger reads from RNA the copy's own LTR made. The shape and the strand of the reads can.

One copy, two ways to get reads

A made-up gene with three exons, and an ERV copy in its first intron. The gene's promoter makes pre-mRNA, which covers the introns thinly, and mRNA, which covers only the exons. The copy's own 5′ LTR can start an RNA that runs to its 3′ LTR. Switch each on and off and watch the reads and the four tests below.

Where the copies sit

Real numbers: every RepeatMasker copy in mouse and human, placed by where it sits relative to protein-coding genes. Copies outside genes cannot be passengers of a gene's RNA; copies in introns pointing against their gene are the ones the strand test can check.

Species
  • Most copies of the young ERVs sit outside protein-coding genes: 81% of IAPEz-int, 92% of MMERVK10C-int and 73% of MERVL-int copies in mouse; 79% of HERVK-int and 83% of HERVH-int copies in human.
  • Most young ERV copies in introns point against their gene: IAPEz-int 1,049 against and 277 the same way, MMERVK10C-int 211 and 35, HERVK-int 35 and 10, HERVH-int 780 and 161. For these, a stranded library can tell the copy's own RNA from the gene's.
  • Host gene here means protein-coding gene. Counting long non-coding RNA genes as well would file copies that make their own RNA as passengers: 1,515 of the 2,406 HERVH-int copies inside any annotated gene overlap a long non-coding RNA gene.
  • Check against a published count: with every gene type, as Kong et al. did, 798 of the 1,620 human L1HS copies here are outside genes, 772 in introns and 50 in exons; they report 951, 654 and 5 of 1,610 with an older, smaller annotation.

Checks before calling an ERV activated

This is the common case, not the exception: "the vast majority of TE-derived RNA-seq reads originate from co-transcription or pervasive transcription" (Lanciano and Cristofari, p. 731). Keep two terms apart: readthrough transcription is a gene's RNA carrying a copy along, while 3′ readthrough is a copy's own RNA running past its end into the DNA beyond (p. 724). Deininger et al. worked their tests out on human L1; the logic carries over to ERVs because it rests on how transcription runs, not on the element.

  1. Look at the reads around the copy, not only the count. Reads that stop at the copy's edges and start inside its 5′ end were made by the copy; reads that run at the same depth through the copy and the intron around it belong to the gene. Deininger et al. found unstranded or whole-cell data "would require extensive manual curation looking at the pattern of expression around each L1 locus". One published correction, a modified TEtranscripts, "reduces the read count of intronic TEs proportionally to the coverage of their surrounding introns" (Lanciano and Cristofari, p. 732).
  2. Use a stranded library. Reads carried by a gene's RNA run the gene's way; on a copy pointing against the gene, reads running the copy's way can only come from the copy. Deininger et al. used the strand to estimate this background, and Lanciano and Cristofari describe the ratio of sense to antisense reads in directional (stranded) RNA-seq for the same purpose (p. 732).
  3. Know which RNA was sequenced. Whole-cell and total RNA keep unspliced pre-mRNA, so introns, and the copies in them, get many more reads: "any RNA sample containing the nuclear component is heavily contaminated with introns" (Deininger et al.); Kong et al. saw more intronic repeat reads in rRNA-depleted than in poly-A libraries. Poly(A)-positive or cytoplasmic RNA "can enrich mature mRNAs and reduce the contribution of intronic TEs or pervasive transcription" (Lanciano and Cristofari, p. 726); single-cell data from isolated nuclei has "a large fraction of intronic reads and may obscure autonomous TE transcription" (p. 733).
  4. Split the subfamily's reads by where the copies sit, and test the copies outside genes. Kong et al. divided each subfamily into exonic, intronic and intergenic copies "To further distinguish autonomous TE expression from co-expression with host genes or intron retention".
  5. Find where the RNA starts. A copy's own RNA starts inside its 5′ LTR; mapping transcription start sites with CAGE or RAMPAGE, or reading active chromatin marks at the copy, "can also help to distinguish autonomous TE transcription from passive TE transcription" (Lanciano and Cristofari, p. 732).
  6. If the host gene is up as much as the copy, suspect a passenger. In the model, switching the gene on raises the copy's count with no step at its edges.
